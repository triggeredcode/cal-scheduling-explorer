What this tells us, and what it doesn’t

For the first example, HB is the number of complete assignments when each of B requests can go to any of H hosts. Greedy host scanning is O(H) per booking, or O(BH) overall. Production’s additional linear filters change constants, not that growth pattern.

These counters use different work units. They are not a timing benchmark, and counting every assignment is not a proof that every exact solver must visit it. Exhaustive search here knows the request list and its objective. A real booking service does not know future arrivals.

Cal.com’s weighted-distribution policy keeps available hosts with the largest weighted booking shortfall, then applies priority and least-recent booking. This explorer isolates the idea of local balancing. It does not simulate routing, weighted credits, travel, external APIs, or production concurrency.

Free-time math is a different job.

Change a busy range. Keep the gaps. Optionally intersect them with a second host’s free window.

Bookable starts

One UTC day, 09:00–17:00. A second busy block is fixed at 13:00–14:30. Endpoints are exclusive. Starts follow a 30-minute grid, and the whole meeting must fit.

Normalize once. Sort once. Sweep sorted intervals. For two sorted lists, intersect in O(m + n). Sorting costs O(n log n); producing the output costs work too.

My takeaway: keep the immediate choice small, reuse interval operations, and measure the whole booking path.
